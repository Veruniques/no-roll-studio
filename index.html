Kiwi.com

Product Design Team Lead · Ancillaries · 2022–2024

Product Design > Strategy, UX/UI, design leadership · Kiwi.com

  • €2 → €25: the goal for ancillary revenue per booking
  • 2×: the goal for overall revenue, with ancillaries at around 50% of the mix
  • 3: platforms: web, responsive, iOS + Android
  • 10+: products: bags, seats, insurance, Guarantee, fare types, bundles…
  • 100s: of screens across search, booking and post-booking

Ancillaries are everything you buy on top of a flight. Bags, seats, AXA insurance, Kiwi.com Guarantee, Price Lock, fare types, service packages, bundles, Booking.com and Rentalcars. Each had its own team and its own idea of where to upsell. The business needed more revenue from it, the company was reorganising ownership mid-flight, and nobody had the full picture.

I took over the design team mid-reorg and co-led the domain with product and engineering. Everything below came out of design jams I ran with designers, PMs, engineers and, regularly, C-level. And a lot of it I designed with my own hands.

Key decisions

  • Decisions kept changing with the evidence: we A/B tested variants and kept what performed, using revenue, CX feedback, UX research and quantitative data
  • One upsell moment per product per stage, triggered by context (first MMB visit, before check-in when bags or seats are still available, after a schedule change), not by banner real estate
  • Business rules live in the design, not in a spreadsheet next to it: Price Lock only shows when the fare margin clears a threshold, different for B2C and B2B2C
  • Friction designed on purpose: the “are you sure” moments were mapped and made consistent instead of each team inventing its own

Selling without tricks

My job was also to keep ancillaries transparent, with no shady practices, in a domain under heavy pressure to grow revenue. One offer per product per stage, triggered by what the traveller was doing. Recommendations shown with the same table people had just seen, so an upgrade read as information, not a trick. The UI never promises what the ticket doesn’t include, and saying no stays clear, like the explicit “no insurance” option.

Booking flow

Search to summary

The core flow every ancillary lives inside: search, trip details, ticket fare, baggage, Kiwi.com services, summary. Consistent across web and native.

Bundles

Light, Comfort, All in

Bundles package bags, seats and flexibility into one choice and one discount. I led the iterations after the MVP: upgrades triggered by intent (pick a bag, get offered the bundle that already includes it), a “finding you discounts” moment tested in three versions, seats in bundles with a colour-coded seat map and a mandatory seat step, and the edge cases nobody wants to discover in production: removing a bundle, adding a passenger after an upgrade, infants, multi-traveller seating.

Fare Families

Airline partnerships, end-to-end ownership

Kiwi.com started selling airlines’ own fare families (Light, Standard, Flex…) for Ryanair and Air France-KLM. Each airline has different rules for bags, seats, changes and refunds. My project from the first sketch to handoff: phase 1 desktop MVP, then responsive and native, including the parts nobody wants to design: refunds, assisted refunds, changes that can’t be done automatically, and the customer-service tool.

Decision story

Fare Families: one problem, the options, and why

The rest of this case shows breadth. This one shows how I think. One feature, from the ticket to the critique to what we took forward.

1 · The brief

August 2023. Ticket DAR-4534, assigned to me, priority highest: prototype the Fare Families use cases so the team understands the flow and its impact before anyone commits engineering time. Airlines were about to sell their own fares through us, and nobody had drawn what that does to a booking that already has bags, seats, Guarantee and service packages in it.

2 · Look outside first

Before touching our flow I benchmarked how airlines and other booking sites present fare families. Two patterns kept coming back: a column table with ticks, where the middle option does the selling, and a nudge to upgrade right before checkout. Cabin tabs and photo-heavy layouts looked great and explained nothing.

3 · The hard part: fare conditions

Fare rules are legal text, different for every airline, and people need them before they pay, not after. Three options on the table: a modal on the overview step, a dedicated conditions page, or an FAQ article with the full airline text. I prototyped the modal in several states, from a short summary to the full rules, and took all three to design critique on 23 January 2024.

4 · Recommending a fare

Two scenarios. Scenario 1: the cheapest fare, or the one that already includes bags. Scenario 2: a higher fare that includes seating. For each, a recommendation at the moment of choice (“Light fare is recommended for your trip”), shown with the same table people had just seen, so the upgrade reads as information, not a trick. Five variants of where and how it appears.

5 · What we took forward
  • Baggage allowance and seat-map states come from the fare attributes, so the UI never promises what the ticket doesn’t include
  • A short summary of conditions in the flow, the full airline text one tap away
  • Recommendations tied to what the traveller already picked, not a blanket upsell
  • Post-booking: “search for a new trip” kept as the main path where data showed high conversion back to search

Insurance & protection

AXA, Kiwi.com Guarantee, AirHelp

Hypothesis: better merchandising of AXA insurance improves product communication and attachment rates. Unbundled products, per-passenger selection, a clear “no insurance” option and policy details people can actually read. I led this stream and co-designed it with the team, together with Kiwi.com Guarantee and AirHelp placements.

Post-booking & email

Components, confirmations, boarding documents

The quiet half of ancillaries: Manage My Booking, transactional emails, e-tickets and boarding documents. Audited and redesigned as one component set used across web, apps and email.

Keboola

Product Design Lead · 2020–2022

Product Design > Data platform, B2B + B2C, web, brand · Keboola

  • 0 → 1: research program and data tracking
  • UI 2.0: dashboard, project overview, data catalog
  • 30+: website pages designed
  • 50+: illustrations and campaign visuals

Keboola is a data platform built by and for data engineers. Then it launched pay-as-you-go for people who are not data engineers. No research, no tracking, no design process. And a UI where Storage, Transformations and Workspaces each behaved differently.

I joined as the brand and web designer and became the product design lead. I built the process first, then the product. And kept the brand and the website, because nobody else was going to.

My role

  • Built data-driven product development from scratch
  • Set up the user research program
  • Worked directly on the design systems for both the product and the website
  • Championed UX against technical constraints
  • Owned the website (Webflow), illustration system and launch campaigns

Testing and funnels

Before development, and after release

I led research interviews on prototypes. We tested features before development and tuned them before handoff to engineering. After release, I led follow-up interviews, collected the data and went through user funnels to see where people dropped off.

Flow Builder

Data pipelines, block by block

Flow Builder let non-technical stakeholders build a data pipeline themselves: drag and drop and a visual system, instead of SQL and a Windows 95 look. Tasks grouped into phases, new configurations created in place, and a jobs view for what actually ran.

Product UI 2.0

Dashboard, jobs, transformations, workspaces

One pattern for columns, search and the three-dot menu across Storage, Transformations and Workspaces. Real use cases mapped (workspace from storage, transformation from workspace, and back) with warnings where users could lose work. Pay-as-you-go onboarding: a dashboard with 8 states that adapts to what the user has already done.

Storage & data catalog

PFLB

Product Design Lead · Load-testing platform and UI kit · 2024–2025

Product design > B2B SaaS, developer tool · PFLB (boomq) · Contract, remote

  • 3: ways to start a test: from scratch, from imported requests, or from JMeter
  • 14: months on contract, working mainly on the product

PFLB is a cloud platform for checking how a website or app handles load before go-live. A test can start from a low-code editor, imported requests or an existing JMeter script, and then needs a load profile, SLAs, a run and a report someone can read.

My co-lead from Kiwi.com was working on the project and brought me into the team. I worked mainly on the platform and the UI kit, and built prototypes. The website was a small part of the job.

My role

  • Product design across the platform, with screens that went into production
  • Work on the PFLB UI kit and visual experiments
  • Prototypes of new flows and iterations
  • A smaller part of the work on the PFLB website

Creating a test

Explorations and iterations

Load profile, use cases and allocation groups, with a live chart of the load shape next to its settings.

Bringing your own JMeter tests

Explorations and iterations

For teams that already have scripts: upload a JMX file with its supporting files, then see the test as settings and containers.

SLAs

Explorations and iterations

SLAs across a test, its transactions and requests, with time ranges and a status for each one.

Anomaly detection

In production

Anomalies marked on the response time chart, with a detection switch and a sensitivity setting per test, and notification settings in the account.

Ordering expert work

A dashboard with credits, trend reports and assets, and a flow for ordering test maintenance or execution from PFLB’s engineers.

UI kit and visual experiments

Explorations

Experiments with navigation, test containers and workload charts, alongside the UI kit.
